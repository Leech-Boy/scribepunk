--- layout: post title: "Getting rid of stuff" subtitle: "My experience using the Kon-Mari tidying method" categories: substack ---

In February or May of this year, a friend from church mentioned that he had done this thing called the Kon-Mari cleaning method. The way he described it was that you take all of your things, one category at a time (clothes, then books, then kitchen items, etc), gather them in one place in your house, pick up each item, and if it does not "spark joy", you get rid of it.

This idea was fascinating to me I. I have always had this feeling that I have too many things, and this has afflicted me with a vague sense of guilt over the wasted money, wasted space, the useless increase in pollution associated with the manufacture and distribution of all these products, and all the cognitive load of keeping a house full of junk tidy.

So, a few days later, I went to my local library and picked up two books by Marie Kondo, The Life-Changing Magic of Tidying Up: The Japanese Art of Declutering and Organizing" and "Spark Joy: an Illustrated Master Class on the Art of Organizing and Tidying up". I read Tidying Up over the course of two or three days; this one is her first book, and it goes through the basics of her process. I resolved to read the relevant chapters of Spark Joy as I got to them in the process; this book is a companion to Tidying up.

My friend from church gave me a generally correct impression of what the Kon-Mari tidying method would entail, but that did not prevent many surprises along the way. Throughout this whole process, I was consistently surprised by Marie's attitude towards things and by my experiences with the instructions.

My first surprise was in how Marie describes inanimate objects. She has a much more animist view of objects than any person I have ever met. She says to treat all of your things with respect because they only want to serve you. The way to get rid of a thing without guilt is to hold it, really look at it and take a moment to thank it for everything it has done for you, then put it on your discard pile. By holding onto something you no longer need, you are preventing that thing from fulfilling it's purpose of serving someone well. She reverses the guilt. Instead of saying that you should feel guilty for having all these things, she says that you can relieve your possession's shame at being unable to fulfill its duty. I think this focus on shame and guilt, though it is not quite as explicit in her writing as I am making it out to be, is I think why this method works so well.

A very small change I made in my kitchen has had a surprisingly calming effect because it has helped me shift my mindset from shame to joy. I have two kinds of dinner plates. I have about 6 plates that are plain and white, and two plates, one red and one yellow, that are a bit heftier and have more of a pattern. In the past I always kept these on the bottom of my stack of plates. I had a vague idea that they should be reserved for "special occassions", but I never had any of those, so what turned out was that I would use them only when I hadn't done the dishes in several days. These plates, which were prettier and more fun than my other plates became a reminder of my neglected chores.

After going through all my kitchen things and getting rid of whatever did not spark joy, I was left with all the same dinner plates as before, but I decided that I should use the prettier plates as often as I could, because they sparked more joy than the plain plates. In some cases this logic will fail, you cannot always choose the more enojoyable route, but in the case of plates there is no harm; I was going to have to wash a plate anyways, whether it was a fun plate or a boring plate makes no difference after the fact. When I have to use a plain, white plate, that still serves as a reminder of dirty dishes, but it is not nearly as disheartening as my situation before. For one thing, it is a reminder of a day or two of dirty dishes, not a reminder of 3+ days of dirty dishes, and for another I don't have the experience of shame in a situation that I should feel joy in.

The other change I made in my kitchen is that I no longer store things on the counter. As Marie Kondo notes, to keep a kitchen clean, you must make the kitchen easy to clean. I previously had several corners of my kitchen counter reserved as storage space. Any time I wanted to clean the counter I had to go section by section, cleaning and drying, while moving the things stored in each section of the counter to a different section. While this wasn't that difficult, it did stop me from cleaning the counter as often as I could have. Now that I don't store anything on my kitchen counter, it is easier to clean, and I clean it more often.

During the process of getting rid of things, I had many surprises regarding what I did and didn't get rid of. I have two instruments, a violin and an electric bass, which at the time I had not played in months. I was half preparing myself to get rid of them, but when I picked them up I did really feel a spark of joy to hold them, and so I kept them. By holding a thing, and really examining how it makes you feel, you can see past what the thing means to you, or maybe more accurately what you want it to be, and you see what it is.

In my mind, my unplayed instruments were objects that had missed their calling. In reality, they were simply instruments that I had the ability to play, that I had not played in a long time. Since that time I have played my bass again (in two live performances no less).

I also had some surprises in getting rid of books and boardgames. I did not expect to get rid of many of either, and the things I did get rid of were not all the things I expected to get rid of. I have several boardgames I've bought at a convention purely because I liked the way the box art looked. They are older games, and the artwork is very retro, even the box shapes identify them as older games (older games tend to have long flat boxes like Monoply, whereas new games tend to have more cubic proportions). Some of these games I haven't even played. When I picked them up, and examined how I felt about them, I realized that just owning them made me happy. There were other games that I had played before, and even enjoyed somewhat, but having them simply didn't spark joy so I got rid of them. I don't even remember all of them, so I think it's safe to say I made the right choice

Likewise, I got rid of more books than I expected to. I've always considered myself a bookish person, and I thought that getting rid of books would be contrary to my nature. In fact, I got rid of a good number of books, about a third of my collection. Some of these were books I started and never finished, some were books I never started, and others were books I had read all the way through, but realized that I would suffer no great loss by not having them. For months before I did this tidying, I had been planning to buy a new bookshelf, because I did not have space for all my books and boardgames. After getting rid of what did not spark joy, I found that I had more than enough bookshelf space (exactly like Marie said I would).

In her companion book Spark Joy, there is a section on folding clothes that I found exceptionally helpful. Essentially the idea is to fold everything into a rectangle (there are detailed tips on how to do this with different kinds of clothes) and store these rectangles upright. You can see everything at once and it is very space efficient. I was able to consolidate my underwear and socks to just one drawer instead of two, leaving the second small drawer free for the things that had previously lived on top of my dresser (more on that later), and I can fit almost every piece of clothing I have in my dresser. Marie says that if you fold properly you don't have to worry about wrinkles, I have not had success with this, so I leave all my collared shirts hanging. Everything else fits in my dresser.

Another purchase I was able to avoid by going through this process was a pocket organizer. Those who knew me in college know that I used to wear a fannypack almost everywhere I went. Since then, the fannypack is mostly retired, though it comes out occasionaly. I stopped wearing it because I don't like having something that bulky at my waist, it got in the way sometimes, and I don't like the way that a cross-body fannypack moves around. I had been considering getting a pocket organizer as a replacement (it's like a glorified wallet), but something Marie said stopped me from buying one. She recommends emptying your bag out every day when you get home. This is not something I'd ever done; I'd always preferred to have a bag that was always packed with whatever I need. By emptying my bag/pockets every day, I have become more conscientious of what I really need to be carrying with me.

I've set aside the drawer that used to be my sock drawer as my place for the things that I might need when I go out. It fills the role that my fannypack used to. Instead of grabbing one bag when I leave in addition to my wallet, keys, and phone, I go to my drawer and grab all the things I expect to need. I have the space split up by object importance; things that I use almost every day are in a section at the front, with less used things more towards the back. I am still working on making sure that I always have what I need (heck, I was working on this back when I carried my fannypack everywhere), but I'm really happy that I found a organizational solution that doesn't require me to buy another product. I carry less, but generally I am able to plan well enough that I always have what I need.

I'm not sure I've bought in totally to everything that Marie has to say about tidying. Without her books on hand, I'm going off of memory, so some of this could be wrong. She claims that a person only ever needs to go through this process once in their life, and then they will have exactly as much stuff as they need, and their number of possessions will stay relatively constant forever. I may be taking this a bit to literally (as I often do), but I find that this has not been true for me. For context, I was living in (and still do live in) what might be considered a "stereotypical male living space", no rugs, dining table, floor lamps, plants, or wall art. I have since acquired a dining table and chairs, and I expect to acquire some of the other things sometime. My DVD collection continues to grow, and I have bought a few books since my purge. I am certainly more conscientious of my things and I think it would be easy now to get rid of things that I don't need, and I no longer feel the same guilt of being a wanton consumer for consumerism's sake.

I expect that I will continue to get more and more things, especially furniture, but I feel equipped to deal with them in case I ever get to feeling like I have too much of the wrong things.

Overall, I had a great experience with this whole process. Getting rid of things was surprisingly easy and fun. Marie's writing is a lot of fun to read; I really enjoy the way that she focuses on the spirit on inanimate objects (maybe that is a contradiction in terms, the anima of an inanimate thing). I would recommend this process to anyone. It doesn't cost anything, likely your local library will have one or both of the books I read, and you may just get a lot of peace of mind out of it.
