--- layout: post title: "Stop Worrying About VA Proposed Amendment 2" subtitle: "On the Separation of Church and State" categories: substack ---

On Tuesday November 3rd, Virginians will be able to vote to approve the following amendment of the State Constitution: Question: Should the Constitution of Virginia be amended to (i) remove the ban on same-sex marriage; (ii) affirm that two adults may marry regardless of sex, gender, or race; and (iii) require all legally valid marriages to be treated equally under the law? I have heard many people say that this is an important question for Christian's to vote on because "the sanctity of marriage is at stake". I think my fellow Christians are failing to understand the significance (or lack thereof) of this question and so I have decided to put in my 2 cents (this may be the first controversial thing I've ever posted).

The bottom line is this, when the Virginia Constitution refers to marriage, it is not referring to the same idea as the Bible does when it refers to marriage. The Bible defines marriage as a union of a man and a woman into "one flesh" (Gen 2:24) which may only be ended as a consequence of sexual immorality (Matt 19:9). The Virginia Constitution will have this to say about marriage if amended: "...This Commonwealth and its political subdivisions shall recognize any lawful marriage between two adult persons and treat such marriages equally under the law, regardless of the sex, gender, or race of such persons."

Both of these documents provide definitions of marriage (except that they don't really, but we'll get into that later) for a certain context. The Virginia State Constitution provides a definition of marriage for the purposes of the law, which is important for the filing of taxes and the distribution of material goods and parental responsibilities in the case of death or divorce, among many other things. The Bible provides a definition of marriage in a spiritual sense. It describes what it means for a couple to be married, albeit not very rigorously. The fact that these definitions are different does not constitue a contradiction or a disagreement, they are two separate concepts that happen to share the same word. For clarity I will use the terms "holy matrimony" and "civil union" for the marriages defined by The Bible, and the Virginia Constituion respectively.

If the definition of marriage in the Virginia State Constitution (i.e. the definition of civil unions in The State of Virginia) were to change after November 3rd, "the sanctity of marriage" will be unaffected. There is nothing holy about a civil union, and so the word "marriage" in the phrase "the sanctity of marriage", must be interpreted to mean holy matrimony. The question then is whether "the sanctity of holy matrimony" will be affected by a change in the definition of civil unions in The State of Virginia. The answer to this should be an obvious no, because they are independent things.

I have often heard the idea from theologically conservative Christians with politically libertarian views that same-sex couples should be allowed to marry, but they should call it something other than marriage. I have two problems with this. First, Christians do not have a monopoly on the definition of the word "marriage". When The Bible defines holy matrimony, it does not use the word "marriage", it uses ancient Greek and Hebrew words that require characters that aren't on my keyboard. The use of the word "marriage" to refer to the concept of holy matrimony is the result of a translator's decision, not the decree of God. Second, this would lead to fewer protections under the law for same-sex couples. Every law that mentions marriage, or married couples would have to be individually amended to also apply to people in a same-sex civil union, or those people would not have the rights and protections offered in that law. Furthermore, there would be nothing stopping lawmakers from passing laws that affect only people in same-sex civil unions, or only in opposite-sex civil unions for that matter.

I think it is important for Christians to be able to make the distinction between civil union and holy matrimony because it gives you the rhetorical tools to tackle some other difficult questions. Several years ago, a college Bible study I was in went on a tangential discussion about divorce. One of the members of the group recounted a conversation he had with his boss, a Christian, who defended the position that a couple can only be divorced on the basis of sexual imorality. The person telling the story raised the concern that this would prevent someone experiencing domestic abuse from getting out of their dangerous situation. I didn't have much to say at the time, but much later I realized that making this distinction between holy matrimony and civil union makes this issue a whole lot cleaner.

A person may be in a civil union, but not a holy matrimony, or vice-versa, or both, or neither. If a Christian in a state of holy matrimony is experiencing domestic violence, I see nothing wrong with them seeking a civil divorce. The victim can protect themself from violence and get the financial resources to support themselves, and if applicable, protect and support their children who may also be in danger. In this example, the couple is not married in the eyes of the state, but they are still married in the eyes of God. This means that if either one gets married to someone else, they will be committing adultery. Not only does this not violate the Bible's comandments about holy matrimony (as far as I am aware), but I believe it also demonstrates a commitment to keep a fellow Christian from sinning. When a person removes themself from a place where their spouse can hurt them, they are taking away that spouse's ability to sin in a particular way, which is a good thing to do. If you stick to the mindset that marriage only ever means one thing then you have a dilemma here, either stay in a dangerous situation to preserve the marriage, or get divorced to protect yourself and your family, sinning in the process. By making this distinction, we reveal the third option; remain in holy matrimony, but obtain a civil divorce, which seems like the best of the 3.

There is one last linguistic tangent I will take before wrapping this up, which is that the texts I've cited don't actually give definitions of marriage, they imply, or even assume them. The Virginia State Constitution does not define how to enter marriage or what exactly marriage entails (though I'm sure there are Virginia laws that lay this out more clearly), it merely defines a right to enter marriage. The scripture I've cited, likewise does not define marriage explicitly. In fact, I'm not even sure it is appropriate to read Matthew 19:9 as "a holy matrimony can only be ended after sexual immorality has occurred". "Whoever divorces his wife, except for sexual immorality, and marries another, commits adultery". A literal reading tells us that a marriage can be ended for any reason, and a new marriage begun, except that that new marriage will be an adulterous one. I'm not really sure what to make of that because I would've considered adulterous marriage to be an oxymoron until I read this passage more carefully.

The point of all of this is to convince my fellow Christians that the proposed Virgnia Amendment 2 is not something to worry about. It is not an attempt to undermine the sanctity of marriage, which is impossible because the sanctity of marriage is established by God. It does not contradict scripture's definition of marriage, because it is referring to a different concept entirely which happens to use the same word. It is an attempt to give same-sex couples the same legal and fiscal rights and responsibilites as other couples. Don't tell Christians that they are required to vote a particular way. This issue is not eternally significant, so don't worry about it too much.
