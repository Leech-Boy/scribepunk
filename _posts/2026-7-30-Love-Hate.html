Earlier this month, I moved into a new apartment that is in a much more remote area than my previous apartment was. I have been waiting for two weeks now for my wifi to be set up. This is not the end of the world. There are many things I can do on my PC and smartphone without internet access, and if I desperately need the internet, I can use my mobile data, or go to a coffee shop or library and use their internet. The important part of this experience has been to make me think about how greedy for resources computers are, and how this doesn't have to be the case.

There are two main categories of resources than computers consume greedily, their own compute power and time, and internet conectivity. When I say that computers are greedy, what I really mean is that computer programs are greedy. The computer itself is a tool. It is designed so that it can be used any way you please. Computer programs are often gadgets (see my article on tools and gadgets), they come with constraints of how they operate, and what they accomplish. My frustration then, is not with computers themselves; it is pointless to be frustrated with a tool. My frustration is with the software that is typically included on computers. They use more of these resources than they need to to get their assigned jobs done.

The issue of internet usage is simpler, so I will start there. While I have been without wifi in my home, there have been certain tasks I have had to do that require internet, for example setting up autopay on my new rent and utilities and checking my email. I would prefer to do these things on my PC, but that would require me to use my phone as a hotspot, as opposed to directly using its mobile data. I'm using mobile data either way, but I noticed that I used more when using my phone as a hotspot.

I assume the reason for this is that my phone is aware that it is using data, so it limits its data usage. The PC only knows that it is connected to the internet, and so it does not try to be frugal. It is possible to tell my PC that it is on a metered connection, which I imagine would solve this issue, but this difference in data usage begs the question of what my computer is actually doing with all that extra data.

I estimate that I have spent about 8-10 times as much time on the internet on my smartphone, than on my PC through the hotspot, but their data usage is just about the same, both using about 6 GigaBytes of data. The two hours or so, I have spent using my phone as a hotspot have cost me more than $40 in extra data charges. So what has my computer been doing with all this data that is so important? I have no idea. As far as I can tell it has performed exactly the same kinds of tasks as my smartphone could, albeit with a larger screen.

This is the greed I was talking about. By default, my PC will use an exorbitant amount of data because the developers of PC software assume that you will have unlimited access to the internet. Another issue that occurs because these developers assume constant and unlimited internet access is that I am unable to play some of my videogames, despite the fact that they are downloaded on my computer, and do not require any internet connectivity in and of themselves. The games this has happened for are games made by Electronic Arts, in my case this is The Sims 4 and Star Wars: Jedi Survivor.

Neither of these games are online games, but because they are EA games, they can only be launched from the EA games launcher, and the EA games launcher requires internet connectivity. After doing some digging on my smartphone (using up my data), I realized that this problem may have been avoided if I had been running the game on Windows like the developers intended. Because I was using Linux, I didn't have access to the "offline" mode of the EA games launcher. The problem here is that the default assumption is that a computer will be connected to the internet constantly (which is not a terrible assumption), and that loss of internet connectivity is an unusual edge case that doesn't require a robust solution (which is the problem).

I, myself am guilty of the assumption that I will always have access to the internet. I have historically treated online file storage as something that I always have. I have files in a Google Drive, many Role-Playing Game pdfs in a DriveThruRPG library, and a few movies I've bought on Amazon Prime Video. After this experience of the last few weeks, I have resolved to rely less on online storage for the files that I care about. This will require me to find a reliable way to back up all these files so they cannot be lost, but that will likely be a topic for another time.

The second way that computers are greedy, is in the way that they use their own resources. Computer programs used to be written to efficiently use memory and compute time, because those were scarce resources. Thanks to Moore's Law, computers have been getting faster, cheaper, and more powerful over the years. As a result, programming languages have been developed with an emphasis on ease of development rather than efficiency, so software developers can now right less efficient programs in less time, leading to a glut of resource hungry computer programs.

First, the anecdotal evidence of this claim. Some old programs were written so efficiently that they have inspired short documentaries in their honor. Off the top of my head (and without internet access), I know of two such examples, the videogame Rollercoaster Tycoon, and the inverse square root algorithm used in one of the Quake videogames. You would be hard-pressed to find a documentary for any such impressive code written today.

The second, and much more obvious symptom of this habit of software development is in the decreasing usefulness of old computers. It is not just that they are less useful than their modern counterparts, or even just that they are wearing down; the software that they run is being continuously upgraded. Older computers cannot run all new software, because new software is not designed with efficiency in mind. This is a problem because computers always assume that you want to install the latest version of a piece of software, which leads old computers to be quickly bloated with programs hungry for resources that they don't have.

This does not have to be the case. Besides my smartphone, I have two computers, a PC, which I am using now, and a laptop which I use when I need a large screen outside the of house. The laptop is about 10 years old or more, and yet, with some effort, I was able to get it to run well enough for ocassional use. I had to install Kali Linux, which has a very lightweight desktop environment, so the graphics don't use up so much processing power, but it doesn't look any worse than any other operating system I've used, and it certainly doesn't look dated. The web browser runs a little slow, but not much worse than any other browser would run with slow internet. I wouldn't be able to run any high end software for video, audio, or image editing, or play games, but otherwise, it does anything I could ask of a computer. All it took was taking a little care in what software I allowed it to install.

The ability to revive an old computer to usefulness requires a bit of research and for most people, it is easier to just buy a new computer. If software developers took a bit more time thinking about how their software would run on low-powered computers, the average consumer would be able to keep their computer longer without an upgrade. Market forces have so far been against this because that would turn "consumers" into "owners" of their computers, and nobody who sells computers would want that.

I am a little optimistic that things will eventually get better though. There is only so much that computers can improve. We are reaching the physical limit of how many transistors we can fit on a chip, RAM costs are skyrocketing due to demand from data-centers, and the rare earth metals that go into computers are a limited resource. I hope that these pressures will push consumers to demand more longevity out of their devices, which could force software developers to start to seriously consider the needs of people with older computers.

In the meantime, treat your computer well, use it as long as you can, buy secondhand whenever possible, remember that you don't own anything that you have stored on the internet, only the things you have stored locally, and if you are going to college to get a computer science degree and they tell you you need a high end computer to do your classes, don't listen to them. You might need one if you are taking a lot of classes on graphics or neural nets, otherwise you'll be fine with any old thing.
