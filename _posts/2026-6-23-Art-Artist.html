--- layout: post title: "Art is for the Artist, not the Audience" subtitle: "And why that matters" categories: substack ---

I once saw a video on YouTube about a group of people who drew beautiful patterns of colored sand, only to destroy them. I believe they were Buddhist monks but can’t be certain I remember correctly. The important thing is that they would spend days or weeks using straw-shaped tools to precisely place different colors of sand on circular boards. Once they had completely filled the board with a beautiful pattern, they overturned the board, destroying the pattern. These art pieces have no audience, besides the creator. This story is important because I believe that many people have an unexamined assumption that the audience is essential to a piece of art. I will explain what lead me to suspect this is an idea that people have, and what I believe the unfortunate consequences of this belief are.

If we view the audience as necessary for art to exist, the beautiful patterns mentioned above are not art. The immediate, pedantic counterargument is that the creator is the audience, but that does not affect the core idea that I am trying to convey, and so I will define “audience” to exclude the creator(s) of the piece of art.

To present a more extreme example, consider some of the poems I have composed. The poems to which I refer are the ones that have never been written down or spoken. They were composed entirely in my own head, and in many cases, promptly forgotten. I am confident that at least one of these poems, which was about a sparrow and the snow, was quite good. Unfortunately, I have no evidence of this, as I have forgotten almost the entire thing, so you will just have to trust me. This poem was beautiful, with a strong rhyming scheme and meter and so I would consider it art, despite the lack of an audience.

In our modern age, we often view art as a product. This is a belief leads to some unfortunate conclusions. Certainly, art can, and should, be enjoyed, but this pastime is not the same as the pursuit of art; it is merely the pursuit of entertainment, or the exercise of art critique or curation. I could list a number of consequences of this view of art as a product, but I have a bone to pick with AI, and I will not allow myself to be distracted.

Generative AI (henceforth simply “AI”) is a technology that promises the ability to effortlessly create beautiful art. This is a lie. The problem is not that AI cannot create something beautiful, nor is it that every piece of AI art has telltale signs that it is AI. Whether a thing is art has nothing to do with the output, and everything to do with the process. If the art-ness of a thing was determined by the end-product then the afore-mentioned Buddhist monks are not creating art. The obvious counterargument here is that you must consider the completed sand pattern to be the piece of art, but this is wrong. In the case of these monks, the destruction is a part of the art. The art is not some thing that exists in a certain moment of time between the end of creation and the beginning of degradation, it is the entire history of the interaction between creator and object.

Why then must AI “art” not be art? Is it not true that there is an interaction between creator and object that requires time and effort to create a piece? This is true, but it misses the point. Those who attempt to create art with AI use AI as their tool because of its convenience. They use it because it gives them an impressive seeming piece of work with very little effort. In other words, they are focused on the product, not on the process, therefore they are not doing art.

Secondly the creation of art requires intention. A piece of toast that shows a picture of The Virgin Mary is not art, unless the toaster did it on purpose. Those who try to make art with AI believe that a thing is art if it is beautiful, otherwise they would not be using it. This is a fundamental misunderstanding of the nature of art. If a person fundamentally misunderstands the nature of art, they cannot create art, except by accident. To create art by accident is a contradiction in terms, because art requires intention.

Besides the issue of AI art, this understanding of art solves another quandary that I have been puzzling over, so I am doubly convinced that it is true. A few months ago, I had a discussion with a fellow member of my church who claimed that the movie “2001: A Space Odyssey” was not art, or at least not good art, because the creators of the movie claimed that “anyone who thinks they ‘got’ the movie was wrong”. I have never seen “2001: A Space Odyssey”, but was compelled to play the Devil’s Advocate on behalf of its creators because I believe a piece of art can have ambiguous meaning and still be good art. Also, I like to stir the pot.

It has been several months since I had this conversation, and I never tried to find the exact text of the disputed quote by the creators, so I may be misrepresenting the argument my friend put forth. I was unable to provide what I felt was a very strong counterargument at the time, but some of the ideas mentioned then became the paragraphs written above. My proper rebuttal is below.

I will assume, that to “get” a movie, means to have the ability to summarize the meaning of the movie into a pithy aphorism, a list of important themes, or some other reduction of the story that strips away the extraneous details. The belief that a story that is not supposed to be “got” must be a bad one, is wrong for two reasons. Firstly, as mentioned above, art is for the artist, art is not for the audience, so the inability of the audience to “get” it is irrelevant. Secondly, if the point of a story is simply to communicate a few ideas that could have been distilled into simpler ones, then “good” stories are always more complex than they need to be.

There is an anecdote about Flannery O’Connor that I heard once that I will relate here. I was unable to verify this story with a quick Bing, so don’t quote me on this. The story goes that someone once asked Flannery O’Connor if she could describe “in a nutshell” the meaning of one of her stories. She replied that if she could have said what she wanted to say “in a nutshell” she would not have written the story.

Anyone who thinks that it is possible to “get” every good story in such a way that there is only one right answer, I would humbly request that that person explains “A Good Man is Hard to Find” by O’Connor, because I don’t “get” it.

I know that art is not meant to be “got” because art is not for the audience. Art is for the artist. If the audience “gets” it, then they should be grateful, but that does not make the art better.

I must clarify an important point, when I say that “art is for the artist”, I mean that “art is for the benefit of the artist”, not that art exists “for the sake of the artist”. If art is not for the sake of the artist, then what is its purpose? Because art is not “for the benefit of the audience”, it must not be true that the purpose of art is communication or entertainment of an audience, though perhaps it may be for communication and entertainment within the artist themself. I don’t want to give the impression that art is just another form of self-care, like cardio or vitamins. Art is more like a sunset or a dog; it won’t keep you alive, but it will keep you out of a straitjacket. You enjoy it and participate in it for the love of it, not for its benefits.

As a consequence of how I have defined art, I am forced to concede that in addition to AI “art”, many other things which are typically called art, are not. Any movie or piece of music which was created as a product, rather than an intentional experience for the artist(s) is not art. This point should not be terribly problematic, as the concepts of “high” vs “low” art are widely understood. As such, I could perhaps concede that AI “art” could be considered “low art”, but only because “low art” is not really and truly art.

In case the point was not driven home adequately elsewhere, I want to reiterate what I see as the core application of this idea is. The fact that (some) people can (sometime/often/always) distinguish between AI writing (or images/videos/songs) and human writing (ditto), has absolutely no impact on any discussion about the moral value of producing art by brain power, because the value of art is in the making, not in the consuming or critiquing.
